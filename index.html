Reading #1: "My website is a shifting house next to a river of knowledge. What could yours be?"

Lauren Schwulst

I've recently become familiar with the concept of a web "shrine." Just a place on a website, perhaps a page or section or even the whole thing, that is dedicated to a subject of one's passion. We are sort of doing this in class, but I want this site I make to serve as an opportunity to infodump about one of my most intense interests besides just writing or art: creepypasta, and the history that comes with it. I want it to host factual information, historical context as well as my own opinions and analyses of different creepypasta, while evoking different eras (perhaps simultaneously?) of spooky internet nostalgia through its presentation. I have attempted this once before with my first coded website, though it was much more personal in aesthetic and I didn't do very much with it. Disorienting tiled gifs, glittery dividers, kind of ugly fonts, using every square inch of the page--I want to coin the term "old internet gothic". Maybe someone has come up with that before me but that's what I want it to feel like. I'd like to be able to embed videos as well, possibly audio as well as animation to make the site feel alive (or like it WAS alive long ago). Old decrepit web and the concept of the web "lifespan" is something very fascinating to me and the subject of creepypasta lends itself super well to that (one of my primary examples is itself a 25 year old website that could be considered both "dead" and "alive").

Reading #2: "A Handmade Web"

J.R. Carpenter

The relationship we have developed with "the web" (which in and of itself sounds like a dated way to refer to the internet) since the explosion of social media has absolutely detached us from the physicality and the possibility that computers and the web seemed to hold in ye olden days. The internet (or the web? I think semantically there's a difference, a very subtle one. The world wide web is a place, the internet is how you access it) used to feel like a place, in a very different way than it does now--that is to say, unnoticeably. I'm a bit wary of the nostalgia trap that can take a hold of you when you reminisce on how much "better" something was back then, when it may just be an effect of age or of having existed with it for so long (and besides, I wasn't even alive for about half of the old web days). But the way that society at large is conditioned and encouraged to interact with the web is undeniably different than it was in the 90s or 2000s.

I do appreciate this essay(?)'s drawing attention to the tactility, the mindfulness and physicality of the old personal website as opposed to the "invisible" design, the sleek and frictionless internet experience that we're all supposed to like so much better. It's vast and it's all so visible (that comment about Facebook users not even knowing they were on the internet kind of chilled me. We gotta warn them) that it's as if the computer, the code and the physical presence of technology isn't even there. Whereas the old web felt vast and unknowable, but like one was wandering through a maze with a blindfold on. "Surfing" the web used to mean something! Exploration, discovery and creation were mandatory if you wanted to have any fun. The more insular, intimate feeling while everything was just starting out is something I wish I could experience (again?).

Reading #3: "Hello, World!"

Taeyoon Choi

The computer as a city...this reminds me of something I've heard someone say about simulation theory, as silly as it sounds: humans have become capable of building, and to an extent replicating entire landscapes and worlds in the "smaller" digital space that can be almost as convincingly "real" and physical as our own world; is it so strange to conclude that our world may also be a simulation, a replication created by beings more advanced and "larger" than us?

I don't personally subscribe to the simulation theory on any level, but what I do take interest in is the human tendency to replicate what we know in any area that we touch. We are so compulsed to create the same structures, to build our cities and try to create more things like us. It's going to sound stupid but my mind goes to the 1:1 scale models of real cities in Minecraft or the "world" project there where thousands of people, on the same world map, tried to build from the ground up an exact replica of all the cities and towns on Earth. Minecraft and other similar games have this done a lot by players actually; working computers built INSIDE the game inside someone's actual computer, and the like. The similarities between the architecture of the computer and the architecture of our daily life as humans is very interesting to me, though at least part of that has to account for the intertwining of architectural, artistic and computational history Choi mentions.

I also want to address the distancing of computer from the human, technology as a whole from the person's understanding. This touches on very similar points to the "Handmade Web" reading as well as what we've generally been discussing in class, which is the intentional obfuscation and mystifying of how technology really works in products and the general space of the web, in favor of a more smooth, immediate, intangible and almost thoughtless experience. We are becoming one with the machine, and it's not because we're choosing to.
